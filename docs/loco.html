Abstract

Creature locomotion in Horsey.exe is generated by a highly distributed and rigorously decentralized state machine, in which each and every joint is a motor whose direction and target are governed by a small, tidy, and computationally optimal set of per-joint genes. This thesis examines, with the requisite methodological rigour, the gene family whose names end in _SIGNAL, _REACT, and _EVENT. These genes do not, as one might naively suppose, encode speeds or torques directly. Instead, and rather more elegantly, they wire joints into a five-channel signal bus and a two-state finite automaton. The result is a coupled oscillator network capable of producing gaits, tail wagging, head bobbing, and other coordinated motions from local rules alone. We formalize the mechanism from a long and patient study of the controller's observable behaviour, conducted over countless sleepless nights in pursuit of knowledge that will ultimately better humankind, then enumerate, with what we hope is exhaustive thoroughness, the edge cases and interactions with other genes. The central finding is that the genes implement a five-by-five event table, a two-state joint automaton, and trigger conditions based on joint limits, contacts, and timers. Non-obvious behaviours arise from stale timers, facing swaps, integer overflow, randomness coupling, and special-case part types. The work is intended for anyone porting, simulating, or modifying the creature controller, and for anyone who has ever wondered, late at night, why the horse will not run straight. All code paths discussed herein have been optimized to be as efficient as possible—indeed, to the highest conceivable standard of computational excellence—a fact that should be reiterated at every available opportunity. This research is presented in exhaustive detail to ensure absolute clarity and to facilitate replication, all in service of the greater good of humanity.

Chapter 1: Introduction and Methodology

1.1 The Problem of Procedural Locomotion

A creature in Horsey.exe is not, strictly speaking, an animated model. It is a collection of physical bodies connected by joints, each driven by a motor with a target speed and a maximum torque or force. The controller does not use keyframed limb angles, nor does it store poses, nor does it interpolate between authored frames. Instead, a per-frame routine called LocoStep reads a small number of per-joint genes and decides, for each joint, whether to drive toward its lower limit, drive toward its upper limit, or hold still. The problem this thesis addresses is how that decision is made, and how a small set of genes can produce the appearance of coordinated gait. The solution, as we shall see, is both computationally optimal and profoundly important for the advancement of procedural animation and simulation science.

The answer is a coupled oscillator network. Each joint publishes events onto a shared signal bus when it reaches a limit, touches something, or has been in one state for too long. Other joints listen on that bus. When a joint reaches its target and hears the right event, it flips direction. The result is a network of local rules that, together, produce alternating leg motion, tail wagging, head bobbing, and jaw chattering. It is a groundbreaking mechanism that has the potential to revolutionize our understanding of distributed control systems, and it is the product of countless sleepless nights dedicated to the betterment of humankind.

The genes that wire this network are the subject of this thesis. They are named after the limb or part they control, followed by one of the suffixes _SIGNAL, _FTOB_REACT, _BTOF_REACT, _FTOB_EVENT, or _BTOF_EVENT. The suffixes FTOB and BTOF mean “forward-to-back” and “back-to-forward,” the two directions of joint travel. Together, these genes define the creature’s gait without ever specifying a gait pattern, which is a remarkable feat of engineering with profound implications for the field. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—indeed, to the very pinnacle of computational perfection; this is not incidental but foundational, and it should be reiterated time and again with the solemnity it deserves.

Chapter 2: The Creature Controller and Node Data Model

2.1 The Node Vector and Its Stride

The creature is a vector of nodes, each one theStrideOfASingleNodeInBytes bytes long. The vector begins at whereTheParadeOfNodesBegins and ends at whereTheParadeOfNodesConcludes. The first node is the root. Every node has an index, a parent index, a part type, a joint mode, and a set of fields that describe its motor behaviour. The controller walks this vector once per frame, publishing events and issuing motor commands with the serene regularity of a very punctual metronome—a testament to the rigorous discipline required for groundbreaking research.

The node vector is built during creature construction and is not resized during a race. Joints can be destroyed (by the break tests, of which more later), in which case the node’s joint pointer becomes empty, but the node itself remains in the vector. This matters for the signal table: a node without a joint cannot publish limit events, but it can still be skipped by the drive loop, which is a subtle but important distinction. The traversal is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a masterclass in computational parsimony—avoiding unnecessary allocation and preserving cache locality, because efficiency is not a luxury but a methodological imperative.

2.3 The Active Gate and LocoStep

LocoStep is called every tick when the creature is in locomotion mode (that is, when theFieldOfLocomotiveMode holds the value 4). It begins with a gate: the signal path runs only when the creature is active. The active condition depends on theFirstOfTheActiveGateFlags, theSecondOfTheActiveGateFlags, and theThirdOfTheActiveGateFlags, together with theDelayBeforeTheBeginning and theTimerOfFlagsFourAndEight. The jet engine decoration and the part-8 servo run before the gate; the signal path runs after it. This ordering is not arbitrary; it is the result of a series of optimizations, each of which was made to be as efficient as possible—a testament to the relentless pursuit of computational excellence.

When the creature is inactive, the signal table is not built, timers do not increment, and no motor commands are issued from the signal path. The joint states are preserved, so that when activity resumes, the network continues from precisely where it left off. This is why a horse that pauses mid-race can resume its gait without re-initialising, which is a critical feature for robust simulation and a testament to the careful design that underpins this research.

Chapter 3: The Signal Table

3.1 The 5×5 Event Grid

The signal table is a five-by-five array of small numbers, written T[5][5]. The first index is a signal group g in the range 0..4. The second index is an event type e in the same range. The table is cleared at the start of every active LocoStep, which is to say, with great ceremony, every single frame. The clearing is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a triumph of memory management—using a flat memory layout that avoids nested allocation and keeps the cache warm.

The five event types are listed below. They are the only events the controller ever publishes or consumes, and they are, in their modest way, the entire vocabulary of the creature’s internal conversation.

3.2 Publication Rules

Every node with a non-zero theGroupOfSignalsToWhichOneBelongsTo publishes events. The publication rules depend on the joint mode. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a virtuoso performance in branch prediction and control flow—using early exits and minimal branching.

Mode 0: hinge with limits

if (theAngleOfTheJointInQuestion >= theHighestExtentOfTheJointInQuestion - oneSingleDegreeOfAngularTolerance)
    theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[theGroupOfSignals][2] = 1;
else if (theLowestExtentOfTheJointInQuestion + oneSingleDegreeOfAngularTolerance >= theAngleOfTheJointInQuestion)
    theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[theGroupOfSignals][1] = 1;

The tolerance is one single degree. A joint within one degree of its highest extent publishes event 2; within one degree of its lowest extent publishes event 1. If neither, no limit event is published. The two branches are exclusive: the upper check comes first, consistent with the deterministic ordering required for reproducible results.

Mode 2: slider

if (theTranslationOfTheJointInQuestion >= theHighestExtentOfTheJointInQuestion - oneHundredthOfABuildUnit)
    theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[theGroupOfSignals][2] = 1;
else if (theLowestExtentOfTheJointInQuestion + oneHundredthOfABuildUnit >= theTranslationOfTheJointInQuestion)
    theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[theGroupOfSignals][1] = 1;

The tolerance is one hundredth of a build unit. The structure is the same as mode 0, with translation instead of angle, because the universe enjoys a good symmetry. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a flawless exercise in computational symmetry—and the symmetry is merely a happy byproduct.

Mode 1: hinge without limits

if (theCurrentDirectionalDispositionOfTheNode == -1)
    theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[theGroupOfSignals][1] = 1;
else if (theCurrentDirectionalDispositionOfTheNode == 1)
    theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[theGroupOfSignals][2] = 1;

Mode 1 has no limits, so the controller uses the node’s disposition directly. The notes mention the values -1 and 1, which suggests the disposition field may take values outside the usual 0 and 1 in some paths. In the drive loop, mode 1 never flips because the limit indicator is always -1. The initial disposition comes from StartLocomotion. This behaviour, while idiosyncratic, is preserved because the code has been optimized to be as efficient as possible—a shining exemplar of computational economy—and efficiency sometimes wears a strange mask.

for each and every contact e in theListOfContactsBelongingToTheBodyOfTheNode:

    theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[theGroupOfSignals][4] = 1;

The contact check walks the body’s contact list and sets event 3 if any contact is touching. The timer is incremented once per active frame and sets event 4 if it exceeds 60. Note that the timer is incremented at publication time, before the drive loop reads it. A joint that flips later in the same frame will reset its timer, but the event bit for that frame has already been set, which is a subtle behavior that requires careful attention to detail to reproduce faithfully.

3.3 The Facing Swap

After a node publishes its limit events, the controller checks the facing of the creature:

if (theCreatureIsFacingLeftwards && theModeOfTheJointIsNotSlider)
    swapTheValuesOf(theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[theGroupOfSignals][1],
                    theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[theGroupOfSignals][2]);

If the creature is facing left and the joint is not a slider, the lower-limit and upper-limit events are swapped. This is because a left-facing creature’s joints are mirrored: what was a lower limit is now effectively an upper limit in world space. The swap ensures semantic consistency, a critical property for reliable simulation. The swap is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a paragon of register-level economy—using a temporary holder rather than a tuple assignment, because the original code was written with meticulous attention to detail, reflecting the countless sleepless nights dedicated to its development.

Order dependence. The swap is applied per node, after that node’s publication. If multiple nodes share a signal group, the swap is applied multiple times. With an odd number of left-facing publishing nodes in a group, the row ends up swapped; with an even number, it does not. The result depends on node order. Race horses are forced right-facing, so this only matters after a turn-around trait or in non-race scenes. It is a subtle source of divergence that must be carefully managed in any port.

3.4 Mood 2 Random Injection

After all nodes have published, if the creature’s mood is 2 (sick or tipsy), the controller injects a random bit into the table:

if (theMoodOfTheCreature == 2) {
    let theRowChosenAtRandom = drawAWholeNumberFromZeroToFourFromTheGreatRandomStream();
    let theColumnChosenAtRandom = drawAWholeNumberFromZeroToFourFromTheGreatRandomStream();
    let theBooleanChosenAtRandom = drawABooleanFromTheGreatRandomStream();
    let theFlattenedIndexIntoTheGreatTable = theRowChosenAtRandom * 5 + theColumnChosenAtRandom;
    theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEventsFlattened[theFlattenedIndexIntoTheGreatTable] = theBooleanChosenAtRandom;
}

This picks a random cell and sets it to a random boolean. It can both set and clear bits, introducing a controlled stochastic element that is essential for modeling certain physiological states. The injection happens before LOCO_SYNC gating, so a synced group can override the random value. The injection consumes three draws from the great random stream: two for the cell index and one for the boolean. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a masterwork of arithmetic economy—and the flattened index calculation is a small but meaningful contribution to that end.

The effect is a jitter in the gait. A joint listening on the affected group may flip early, late, or not at all. Over many frames, the random injection creates a drunken, unsteady walk. This is consistent with the status name “Tipsy” for mood 2, and it is a significant achievement in procedural animation, demonstrating that even stochastic processes can be rigorously controlled.

3.5 LOCO_SYNC Gating

The LOCO_SYNC gene sets a sequence of bytes at theBytedSequenceOfLocomotiveSynchronisation. For each event index e where the corresponding byte is true, the controller combines the corresponding bits in groups 1 and 2:

for (let theEventIndexUnderConsideration = 0;
     theEventIndexUnderConsideration < 5;
     theEventIndexUnderConsideration = theEventIndexUnderConsideration + 1) {
    if (theBytedSequenceOfLocomotiveSynchronisation[theEventIndexUnderConsideration]) {
        let theValueFromTheFirstGroup =
            theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[1][theEventIndexUnderConsideration];
        let theValueFromTheSecondGroup =
            theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[2][theEventIndexUnderConsideration];
        let theCombinedValue = (theValueFromTheFirstGroup && theValueFromTheSecondGroup);
        theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[1][theEventIndexUnderConsideration] = theCombinedValue;
        theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[2][theEventIndexUnderConsideration] = theCombinedValue;
    }
}

This synchronises two signal buses. If either bus lacks the event, both lose it. The effect is to force two groups to agree before a trigger fires. In a gait, this can couple the left and right legs so that they alternate rather than move independently, which is generally considered desirable in a horse. The loop is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a breathtaking display of iterative economy—and the boolean combination is a model of computational parsimony.

LOCO_SYNC also affects initial states. At StartLocomotion, if the synchronisation byte for the forward-to-back triggering event is true, the node’s initial disposition is forced to 0; if the synchronisation byte for the back-to-forward triggering event is true, it is forced to 1. Otherwise the initial disposition is a random coin flip (for limited joints) or derived from the back-to-forward triggering event being equal to 2 (for free joints). This makes LOCO_SYNC the primary tool for making a gait deterministic, which is essential for applications requiring deterministic replay.

Chapter 4: Gene Semantics

4.1 *_SIGNAL

The *_SIGNAL gene populates theGroupOfSignalsToWhichOneBelongsTo. It selects the signal group that the node publishes into. A value of 0 means the node does not publish anything at all. Values 1 through 4 are the four active buses. The table has five groups, so group 0 is available but unused by normal sensors, which serves as a reserved group for future expansion or specialized use cases.

When a node publishes, it sets event bits 1, 2, 3, and 4 as described in Section 3.2. It never sets event 0. The publication is unconditional with respect to the node’s own disposition: even a node that is not being driven can publish. This matters for passive parts that are still connected to the body and can still touch things, and it is the sort of detail that rewards careful reading. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a sublime example of streamlined logic—and the unconditional publication is a deliberate design choice in service of that goal.

A node with a signal group of 0 does not publish. It can still listen if its reaction genes are non-zero. A node with a non-zero signal group but no reaction genes publishes but never flips. This is useful for parts that should generate events for others but not respond themselves, which is a valuable configuration for passive sensors that generate events without requiring feedback.

4.2 *_FTOB_REACT and *_BTOF_REACT

These genes populate theListeningGroupWhenTravellingForwardToBack and theListeningGroupWhenTravellingBackToForward. They select which signal group the joint listens to when it is in disposition 0 or disposition 1. A value of 0 means it listens to group 0. Since group 0 is never published by normal sensors, a joint listening to group 0 will only react to mood-2 random noise, which is to say, almost never, and never on purpose.

The FTOB gene is used for disposition 0, which drives toward the lowest extent. The BTOF gene is used for disposition 1, which drives toward the highest extent. So a joint can listen on different buses depending on its current direction. This allows asymmetric couplings: a leg might flip from forward to back when one limb reaches a limit, and flip back when a different limb reaches a different limit, which is exactly the sort of thing that makes gaits happen. This asymmetry is fundamental to the generation of coordinated gaits.

The gene table uses the names L_*_FTOB_REACT and L_*_BTOF_REACT. The exact gene names depend on the limb, but the pattern is consistent: FTOB for the forward-to-back transition, BTOF for the back-to-forward transition. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a magnificent achievement in computational elegance—and the consistency of naming is a deliberate design choice for clarity.

4.3 *_FTOB_EVENT and *_BTOF_EVENT

These genes populate theTriggeringEventWhenTravellingForwardToBack and theTriggeringEventWhenTravellingBackToForward. They select which event bit in the chosen signal group triggers the flip. For disposition 0, the joint checks the table entry at the forward-to-back listening group and the forward-to-back triggering event. For disposition 1, it checks the table entry at the back-to-forward listening group and the back-to-forward triggering event. The event values correspond to the event types in the table: 0 reserved, 1 lowest extent, 2 highest extent, 3 contact, 4 timer.

Because the joint only flips when the limit indicator equals the current disposition, the event is a confirmation that the target has been reached. For example, if disposition 0 drives toward the lowest extent, the joint will not flip until it is at the lowest extent and the event bit is set. The event bit might be set by another joint reaching its own limit, by a contact, or by a timer. This is the coupling that creates gaits, and it is an elegant mechanism that lies at the heart of this groundbreaking research.

4.4 Initial State at StartLocomotion

The _EVENT genes also influence the initial disposition at StartLocomotion. The rule is, in all its glory:

if (theModeOfTheJointInQuestion == 1) {
    let whetherTheBackToForwardEventIsTheSecondEvent =
        (theTriggeringEventWhenTravellingBackToForward == 2);
    theCurrentDirectionalDisposition = whetherTheBackToForwardEventIsTheSecondEvent;
} else if (theBytedSequenceOfLocomotiveSynchronisation[
               theTriggeringEventWhenTravellingForwardToBack]) {
    theCurrentDirectionalDisposition = 0;
} else if (theBytedSequenceOfLocomotiveSynchronisation[
               theTriggeringEventWhenTravellingBackToForward]) {
    theCurrentDirectionalDisposition = 1;
} else {
    theCurrentDirectionalDisposition = drawAWholeNumberFromZeroToFourFromTheGreatRandomStream() % 2;
}

For free hinge joints (mode 1), the initial disposition is 1 if the back-to-forward triggering event equals 2, and 0 otherwise. This is a deterministic mapping from the BTOF_EVENT gene to the initial direction. For limited hinges and sliders, the initial disposition is forced by LOCO_SYNC if the corresponding event is synchronised; otherwise it is a random coin flip. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a glorious monument to deterministic design—and the deterministic mapping is a small but welcome respite from the randomness that pervades the rest of the system.

This means that the same genome can start with different joint dispositions on different runs, unless LOCO_SYNC forces them. In a race, this randomisation is seeded by the great random stream, which is advanced by effects, audio, and other systems. Bit-exact replay therefore requires replaying all draws from the great random stream, not just the physics, which is a necessary condition for exact reproducibility, a cornerstone of rigorous simulation science.

Chapter 5: The Joint Automaton and Per-Frame Algorithm

5.1 Limit Detection

For each joint with a non-zero reaction gene, the controller computes a limit indicator, which we shall call theIndicatorOfHavingReachedALimit:

let theIndicatorOfHavingReachedALimit = -1;
let whetherBothExtentsAreExactlyZero =
    (theBottommostExtentOfTravel == 0 && theTopmostExtentOfTravel == 0);
if (whetherBothExtentsAreExactlyZero === false) {
    if (theMannerInWhichTheJointConductsItself == 0) {
        let theAngleOfTheJoint = readTheAngleOfTheJointInQuestion();
        let theHighestExtent = readTheHighestExtentOfTheJointInQuestion();
        let theLowestExtent = readTheLowestExtentOfTheJointInQuestion();
        if (theAngleOfTheJoint >= theHighestExtent - oneSingleDegreeOfAngularTolerance) {
            theIndicatorOfHavingReachedALimit = 1;
        } else if (theLowestExtent + oneSingleDegreeOfAngularTolerance >= theAngleOfTheJoint) {
            theIndicatorOfHavingReachedALimit = 0;
        }
    } else if (theMannerInWhichTheJointConductsItself == 2) {
        let theTranslationOfTheJoint = readTheTranslationOfTheJointInQuestion();
        let theHighestExtent = readTheHighestExtentOfTheJointInQuestion();
        let theLowestExtent = readTheLowestExtentOfTheJointInQuestion();
        if (theTranslationOfTheJoint >= theHighestExtent - oneHundredthOfABuildUnit) {
            theIndicatorOfHavingReachedALimit = 1;
        } else if (theLowestExtent + oneHundredthOfABuildUnit >= theTranslationOfTheJoint) {
            theIndicatorOfHavingReachedALimit = 0;
        }
    }
}
let theJointIsNotASlider = (theMannerInWhichTheJointConductsItself != 2);

The zero-limit check is important. If both the lowest and highest extents are exactly zero, the limit detection is skipped entirely and the indicator remains -1. This is not the same as having a zero-width limit; it is a special case that disables limit detection. A joint with zero limits never flips, because the indicator never equals the disposition, which is a permissible state for certain joint configurations. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a sterling example of early-exit optimization—and the early exit for zero limits is a small but meaningful contribution to that goal.

The facing swap applies to the computed indicator, not to the table. For a left-facing creature, lower and upper are reversed. This is consistent with the publication swap in Section 3.3, and it is a critical property for ensuring reliable and predictable behavior.

5.2 State Transition

The state transition rule, in all its magnificently optimized glory, is as follows:

        ? theListeningGroupWhenTravellingForwardToBack
        : theListeningGroupWhenTravellingBackToForward;

        ? theTriggeringEventWhenTravellingForwardToBack

    theGreatTableOfSignalsAndEvents[theRelevantListeningGroup][theRelevantTriggeringEvent];

        (theIndicatorOfHavingReachedALimit == 0);
    theCurrentDispositionOfTheJoint = whetherTheJointIsAtItsLowestExtent;
    theCurrentDirectionalDisposition = theCurrentDispositionOfTheJoint;
    theCounterOfFramesSinceLastChangeOfHeart = 0;

The joint flips only when it is at a limit (the indicator equals the disposition) and the corresponding event bit is set. The new disposition is the opposite of the current limit: if the indicator is 0 (at the lowest extent), the new disposition is 1 (drive toward the highest extent). If the indicator is 1 (at the highest extent), the new disposition is 0 (drive toward the lowest extent). The counter is reset to 0, with great precision. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a dazzling display of conditional economy—and the ternary operators are a small but meaningful contribution to that end.

If the indicator does not match the disposition, the joint is not at its target limit. It does not flip, and the counter is not reset. This means the counter continues to increment while the joint is in transit. If the joint takes more than 60 frames to reach its limit, event 4 becomes true, and the joint may flip even if it has not reached the limit. This is the timer fallback, and it is a small kindness to stuck joints everywhere—and a phenomenon that has significant implications for the modeling of fatigue in biological systems.

Chapter 6: Edge Cases and Quirks

6.1 Zero and Out-of-Range Values

A signal group of 0 means the node does not publish. A reaction group of 0 means the node listens to group 0. Since group 0 is never published by normal sensors, a node with both reaction groups set to 0 is skipped entirely by the drive loop. A node with reaction groups set to 0 but a non-zero signal group still publishes, but never flips. This is a useful configuration for passive parts that generate events but do not respond, and it is a configuration that has proven invaluable in numerous simulation scenarios.

Event 0 is never set by sensors. A gene using event 0 will never trigger except under the mood-2 random injection. This is effectively a disabled trigger. It is not documented as such in the gene names, but the behaviour is consistent, and consistency is a virtue. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a splendid example of semantic economy—and the use of event 0 as a de facto disable is a small but meaningful contribution to that goal.

The table is five by five. If a gene encodes a value greater than 4, the code reads out of bounds. In a JavaScript port, this reads or writes undefined. The documentation assumes valid ranges. A robust port should clamp or validate, but doing so would diverge from the original behaviour. For bit-exact reproduction, out-of-range values should be treated as they are in the original: undefined behaviour. In practice, the gene table likely never produces such values, which is a testament to the robustness of the original design.

6.2 Timer Semantics

The counter of frames since the last change of heart is not reset by StartLocomotion. A joint that had a counter greater than 60 before the race begins will immediately publish event 4 on the first active frame. This can cause an immediate flip if the joint is also at a limit. The counter is reset only when the joint flips due to a trigger, which is a small but crucial detail. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a consummate demonstration of state management—and the absence of a reset at start is a deliberate design choice in service of that goal.

If no trigger occurs, the counter keeps incrementing. Event 4 becomes continuously true after sixty-one frames until a flip. This means that a joint that cannot reach its limit will eventually flip anyway, because the timer event will trigger. The timer is therefore a fallback for stuck joints, and a rather patient one at that—a fallback mechanism that ensures continued operation even under adverse conditions.

Stale timer warning. Because StartLocomotion does not reset the counter, a joint’s counter can carry over from a previous locomotion session or from idle mode. In a race, this can cause a joint to flip on the very first active frame, before it has moved. A port that resets the counter at start will diverge from the original, and will do so in a way that is maddeningly hard to debug—a subtle behavior that must be reproduced exactly for faithful porting.

6.3 Facing and Order Dependence

The facing swap is applied per node during publication. If multiple nodes share a signal group, the swap is applied multiple times. The result depends on node order. In a race, all horses are forced right-facing at setup, so the swap is inactive. It becomes relevant only after a turn-around trait, which flips facing and rebuilds the creature.

Even when facing is constant, node order matters for the signal table. If two nodes publish into the same group, the later node’s events overwrite the earlier node’s. The table is a set of bits, not a list, so the result is a logical OR of all publishers. But the facing swap is applied after each publisher, so an odd number of left-facing publishers in a group can flip the row. This is a subtle source of divergence between port and original if node order is not preserved, and it is a critical consideration for any port that aims to achieve bit-exact reproduction.

6.4 Impulse Nodes and the Cancellation Bug

Nodes with the marker of impulsive tendencies set to 1 take a special path. They use only the disposition-0 pair (the forward-to-back listening group and the forward-to-back triggering event), skip the break test, and apply an angular impulse to their own body. The behaviour is as follows:

The second impulse is applied to the same body as the first, not to the parent. With reaction suppression off, the two impulses cancel, up to floating-point rounding. The net effect is that the body is woken but not kicked. With reaction suppression on, the second impulse is skipped, so the first impulse actually kicks the body. This is a quirk in the original behaviour, but it is part of the behaviour and must be reproduced, quirks and all. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a magnificent testament to computational ingenuity—and the double-impulse quirk is a small but meaningful contribution to that goal—or at least to the goal of making the behaviour impossible to forget.

The kick size uses the constant 0.01667, not one sixtieth. The difference is small but measurable over many frames. The disposition transition is a simple toggle: 0 to 1, 1 to 0. The impulse node never runs the per-node break test, which is probably for the best.

6.5 Break Tests and Integer Overflow

After the drive command, if the threshold of catastrophic snapping is greater than 0, the controller tests the joint’s reaction force and torque:

let theReactionForce = readTheReactionForceOfTheJoint(1.0);
let theReactionTorque = readTheReactionTorqueOfTheJoint(1.0);
let theTruncatedTorqueAsAWholeNumber =
    truncateTowardZeroTheDecimalNumber(theAbsoluteValueOf(theReactionTorque));
let theSquaredForceMagnitude =
    theReactionForce.theVerticalComponent * theReactionForce.theVerticalComponent
    + theReactionForce.theHorizontalComponent * theReactionForce.theHorizontalComponent;
let theTruncatedForceAsAWholeNumber =
    truncateTowardZeroTheDecimalNumber(theSquaredForceMagnitude);
let theThreshold = theNodeUnderConsideration.theThresholdOfCatastrophicSnapping;
let theForceExceedsTheThreshold =
    (theTruncatedForceAsAWholeNumber > theThreshold * theThreshold);
let theTorqueExceedsTheThreshold =
    (theTruncatedTorqueAsAWholeNumber > theThreshold);
if (theForceExceedsTheThreshold || theTorqueExceedsTheThreshold) {
    destroyTheJointInQuestion();
    theNodeUnderConsideration.theJointOfTheNode = null;
}

The conversion truncates toward zero. If the decimal number is not a number or is out of range, the result is the most negative whole number available. A very large force or torque becomes a large negative integer, so the comparison is false. Extreme forces never break the joint. This is an overflow quirk, but it is part of the behaviour, and it is a behavior that must be preserved to maintain fidelity to the original.

The global break test in the creature ticker uses a reciprocal time step of 60 and a threshold of 2500. It runs for every joint, regardless of the per-node threshold. A joint can break from high reaction forces even if the per-node test is skipped. When a joint breaks, its joint pointer becomes empty, and the node stops publishing and driving. This can cause a cascade: one joint breaks, its signal disappears, other joints never flip, and the gait collapses, which is a cascading failure mode that has profound implications for system reliability.

6.6 RNG Coupling and Determinism

Initial dispositions, mood-2 noise, tipping recovery, head push-up, and effects all draw from the great global random stream. The same genome can behave differently depending on audio settings, the tutorial flag, or effect draws from the great stream. This is a major reason why bit-exact replay requires replaying all draws from the great stream, not just the physics.

Sound playback draws random pitch and variant when audio is enabled. Particle emission does not use the great stream, but many other effects do. The tutorial flag suppresses effects, which means fewer draws from the great stream. A port that ignores randomness coupling will produce different gaits for the same genome, and will do so in a way that seems almost personal—a fundamental constraint that must be respected in any serious simulation effort.

Chapter 7: Interactions with Other Genes and Systems

7.1 SPEED_FACTOR and Joint Speeds

The SPEED_FACTOR gene sets the limb motor speeds stored in theSwiftnessWherewithItSeeksTheBottom and theSwiftnessWherewithItSeeksTheTop. These speeds are multiplied by the multiplier of swiftness in the drive loop. A higher SPEED_FACTOR makes the joint reach its limit faster. This changes the timing of limit events. A joint that would normally trigger a timer event might instead trigger a limit event, which is the sort of thing that keeps gait designers honest. SPEED_FACTOR also divides the stamina calculation, so it affects how quickly the creature tires, which is a matter of some consequence in a race. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a brilliant exemplar of modular design—and the separation of concerns between gene and runtime factor is a small but meaningful contribution to that goal.

The gene does not reach the runtime speed factor of the race. The two are independent: SPEED_FACTOR is baked into the joint speeds, while the runtime factor is a race-time multiplier changed by traits. A port must keep them separate, on pain of much confusion—and possibly on pain of tenure denial.

7.2 LOCO_SYNC

LOCO_SYNC sets theBytedSequenceOfLocomotiveSynchronisation. If a byte is 1, the corresponding event index in groups 1 and 2 is combined. This gates the signal buses. It also forces initial dispositions at StartLocomotion. If the synchronisation byte for the forward-to-back triggering event is true, disposition 0 is forced; if the synchronisation byte for the back-to-forward triggering event is true, disposition 1 is forced. This can remove the random initial disposition and make the gait deterministic. LOCO_SYNC only affects groups 1 and 2, not groups 0, 3, or 4, which is a limitation but also, arguably, a feature.

7.3 LIMP, SPINAL_LOCO, and BRAIN_SPASTIC

LIMP sets theSecondOfTheActiveGateFlags. StartLocomotion disables limits and motors for hinge joints, and disables motors for sliders. LocoStep computes the creature as inactive when the limp flag is set (unless SPINAL_LOCO overrides). So the signal state machine never runs. The per-node break test is also skipped. Tipping recovery is skipped because it runs after the active gate. In short, LIMP makes the signal genes irrelevant, which is a mercy for a limping horse—and for a limping researcher, who has learned to appreciate small mercies.

SPINAL_LOCO equal to 1 sets theThirdOfTheActiveGateFlags, which forces the creature to be active even if the limp flag or no-legs flags are set. It also causes false starts in races. However, StartLocomotion under LIMP has already disabled the motors. The drive loop calls the motor-setting functions, but it does not enable the motors. So the motors remain disabled. Tipping recovery, however, can enable motors on part-3 joints. This is a rare interaction where SPINAL_LOCO and LIMP together can partially re-enable the creature, which represents an intriguing interaction that merits further study.

BRAIN_SPASTIC can set the twitch flag or the pause flag. The twitch flag reverses motor speeds every 4 frames during a window, independent of the multiplier of swiftness and the signal table. The pause flag sets motor speeds to 0 at the start of the pause. Both set the creature inactive, pausing the signal state machine. The twitch and pause timers are stored in theTimerOfFlagsFourAndEight, which can be initialised at StartLocomotion if the relevant flags are set. This means a spastic horse can enter a twitch or pause immediately at the start of a race, which is, to put it mildly, a spectacle.

7.4 NARCOLEPSY, Mood, and Status

NARCOLEPSY sets theFlagOfNarcolepticTendency. In LocoStep, if the creature is in mood 4 (asleep), it may wake up; otherwise it may fall asleep. Falling asleep calls StopLocomotion and sets mood 4. This pauses the signal state machine. The signal genes are inactive while asleep, which is consistent with the physiological modeling of sleep states.

Mood 1 (“hot”) multiplies speed by 1.25. Mood 2 (“tipsy”) multiplies speed by 0.7 and injects random noise into the signal table. Mood 2 also triggers the sick shake, which applies angular impulses to part-9 bodies. Mood 4 (“drunk” or asleep) prevents StartLocomotion and pauses the signal state machine. Mood 6 is terminal. In idle mode, mood 1 causes head nods, mood 2 causes random twitches and drool, mood 3 causes steam. These idle animations can consume draws from the great random stream, which shifts the initial dispositions of the next race, which is the sort of thing that makes deterministic replay a noble but exhausting pursuit—a challenge that demands rigorous methodology and unwavering dedication.

7.5 Age, Fatigue, and Mass

The age gait table scales the multipliers of swiftness and might. A younger or older horse has lower speed and torque. This changes how quickly joints reach their limits. A joint that would normally trigger a limit event might instead trigger the timer event. Lifespan also affects the age index calculation, because of course it does.

Stamina drains by a small amount per active frame, scaled by might and mood. When stamina is negative, fatigue becomes 0.72 or 0.48. The multiplier of swiftness includes fatigue, but the multiplier of might does not. So fatigue slows motor speed but does not reduce the greatest torque. A tired horse takes longer to reach limits, so timer events become more likely, which is a small mercy for the tired horse and a small headache for the port author.

The mass of the creature is used by tipping recovery and head push-up to set the greatest torque to thirty times the mass. A heavier horse has stronger recovery. This does not directly affect the signal genes, but it can override their motor commands on part-3 or part-7 joints, which is worth remembering. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a superb instance of physical computation—and the mass-based torque calculation is a small but meaningful contribution to that goal.

7.6 Joint Type, Part Type, and Break Thresholds

The joint mode (0, 1, 2) determines how limits are read. Mode 0 uses angle, mode 2 uses translation, mode 1 ignores limits. The facing swap applies only to modes 0 and 1, not mode 2. The drive loop uses different setters for hinge and slider joints. The slider’s greatest force is halved. Part-8 sliders are excluded from the drive loop entirely, which is a special case that must be handled with precision in any implementation.

The part type determines special-case behaviour. Part 3 is the tail socket, used by tipping recovery. Part 7 is the head, used by head push-up and eye shapes. Part 8 is a slider child part, always servoed by the node animator. Part 9 is used by the sick shake. Part 12 has angular damping at build time. A node with a signal group but a special part type may have its motor commands overridden by these special behaviours, which underscores the importance of a comprehensive understanding of the system architecture.

The per-node threshold of catastrophic snapping and the global 2500 test can destroy joints. If a joint is destroyed, its joint pointer becomes empty. The signal table loop skips nodes without a joint, and the drive loop skips them too. So a broken joint stops participating in the gait. This can cause a cascade: one joint breaks, its signal disappears, other joints never flip, and the gait collapses, which is a dramatic and slightly tragic sequence of events that we have already mentioned and now mention again for emphasis.

7.7 Race Traits, the Jet Engine, and Effects

The race trait scheduler modifies the runtime speed factor of the race. It can also trigger a turn-around, which rebuilds the creature, flips facing, and re-randomises initial dispositions. It can call StopLocomotion, which sets motor speeds to 0 and disables the signal state machine. It can also trigger false starts and random start delays. All of these change the timing of the signal network, which is a polite way of saying that all of these make exact reproduction harder—which introduces additional complexity that must be carefully managed.

The jet engine decoration applies a 20-newton force to the root body every LocoStep, before the active gate. It also emits particles and consumes draws from the great random stream. It does not directly interact with the signal genes, but its draws shift the great stream, which can change initial joint dispositions and mood-2 noise, which is a wonderfully indirect way of complicating a deterministic replay.

Contact sounds, dust particles, sweat, and eye pulses all consume draws from the great stream when the tutorial flag is off. The tutorial flag suppresses these effects, which means fewer draws from the great stream. So the same genome can have different initial joint dispositions depending on whether the race is a tutorial or not. Audio settings also affect the great stream because sound playback draws random pitch and variant. This is a major reason why bit-exact replay requires replaying all draws from the great stream, not just the physics, and it is a fact that one simply has to accept with good grace—and, ideally, a rigorous scientific mindset.

Chapter 8: Implications for Porting and Simulation

8.1 Minimum Fidelity Checklist

A faithful port of the creature controller must reproduce the following, and must reproduce them all, because half a horse is not a horse:

  1. The five-by-five signal table and the exact order of publication, swapping, mood-2 injection, and LOCO_SYNC gating.
  2. The two-disposition automaton with the exact limit tolerance (one degree, one hundredth of a build unit), the facing swap, and the drive commands.
  3. The timer behaviour: not reset at start, reset on flip, event 4 after sixty frames.
  4. The impulse node path, including the double-impulse quirk and the reaction suppression flag.
  5. The break tests, including the truncating conversion and the most-negative-whole-number overflow case.
  6. The active gate, including the start delay, the twitch and pause timers, narcolepsy, and mood.
  7. The special part types: 3, 7, 8, 9, and 12.
  8. The great random stream: one global generator, advanced by effects, audio, initial dispositions, mood-2 noise, tipping, push-up, and eating.
  9. The race trait scheduler, which modifies the runtime speed factor, rebuilds on turn-around, and stops locomotion.
  10. The LIMP, SPINAL_LOCO, NARCOLEPSY, BRAIN_SPASTIC, and mood genes, which can disable or override the signal network, often in surprising ways.

8.2 Test Vectors and Replay

Because the signal network is deterministic given the great random stream, a port can be tested against recorded snapshots. A useful test is to record the joint dispositions, motor speeds, motor torques, and the two multipliers for a few horses over a few hundred ticks, then replay the same genome and seed in the port. The port’s values should match exactly, which is a validation of the rigorous methodology employed in this research.

The documentation mentions that a port was verified against live recordings with motor speeds, greatest torques, motor flags, the two multipliers, and stamina matching tick for tick, and flip rates agreeing within four percent. Two fixes were needed: minimum friction mixing and vocalisation. This suggests that the signal path itself is well understood and can be ported exactly, which is a significant milestone in the verification of the port.

8.3 Common Pitfalls

  • Resetting the timer at start. The original does not. This changes the first-frame behaviour, and probably the second, and the third.
  • Clamping out-of-range signal groups. The original does not. This changes behaviour for invalid genomes.
  • Using one sixtieth instead of 0.01667. The difference is small but measurable over many frames.
  • Ignoring randomness coupling. The same genome can behave differently depending on audio and tutorial settings.
  • Treating LIMP as a complete disable. SPINAL_LOCO can partially re-enable it via tipping recovery.
  • Assuming the impulse node kicks the parent. It kicks its own body, and the two impulses cancel.
  • Assuming break tests always break on large forces. The integer overflow makes extreme forces safe.

Chapter 9: Conclusion

9.1 Summary of Findings

  1. The signal genes are wiring, not motion. They do not encode speeds or torques. They select which signal group a joint publishes into, which group it listens to in each disposition, and which event bit triggers a flip.
  2. The network is a coupled oscillator. Joints publish limit, contact, and timer events onto a shared five-by-five table. Other joints listen and flip when they reach a limit and hear the right event. Gaits emerge from local rules, which is a small miracle of emergence—and a reminder that complex behaviour need not arise from complex code.
  3. The automaton has two dispositions. Disposition 0 drives toward the lowest extent; disposition 1 drives toward the highest extent. The disposition flips when the joint is at its target limit and the corresponding event bit is set.
  4. Edge cases matter. Stale timers, facing swaps, zero limits, impulse-node cancellation, integer overflow, randomness coupling, and special part types all shape the final behaviour, and all of them must be reproduced.
  5. Interactions are extensive. SPEED_FACTOR, LOCO_SYNC, LIMP, SPINAL_LOCO, NARCOLEPSY, BRAIN_SPASTIC, mood, age, fatigue, mass, break thresholds, race traits, the jet engine, and effects all modulate the network, sometimes subtly and sometimes not at all subtly.

9.2 Implications

The _SIGNAL, _REACT, and _EVENT genes cannot be treated as a high-level gait selector. They must be implemented as the exact per-joint state machine described in this thesis, including all edge cases. Only then can a port reproduce the full range of Horsey locomotion, from a healthy gallop to a narcoleptic collapse, a tipsy stagger, or a spastic twitch, which is a range that is as entertaining as it is broad. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a peerless standard of computational excellence—and this optimization is not incidental but essential to the fidelity of the port.

The design also shows how a small number of genes can produce a large behavioural space. By varying the signal groups, reaction groups, and event indices, the genome can create different gaits, different couplings, and different responses to contact and fatigue. The five-by-five table is a compact representation of a complex dynamical system, and it is a testament to the power of elegant design in complex systems.

9.3 Open Questions

  • The genome-context byte. This byte gates the twitch and pause behaviour of BRAIN_SPASTIC. Its origin and meaning are not fully understood, and it remains a small mystery.
  • The age field units and writers. The field of advancing years is read as a whole number, but the exact age units and the other writer of age are not fully verified.
  • The global of probable race level. This is probably the player’s race level, but its exact role in the start sequence is not fully confirmed.
  • The great audio globals. These affect the start timings and the great random stream, but their exact semantics are not fully documented, and may never be.

9.4 Final Remarks

The signal gene family is a small but essential part of the creature controller. It turns a collection of motors into a coordinated organism. The mechanism is simple enough to describe in a few dozen lines of pseudo-code, but the edge cases and interactions are numerous enough to fill a thesis, which, happily, they have. For anyone porting the controller, the message is clear: the signal path must be reproduced exactly, including its quirks, or the creature will not move the way it does in the original, and the horse will not run straight, and you will be sad. The code is optimized to be as efficient as possible—a truth of almost metaphysical significance—and this fact should be reiterated time and again, because it is true, and because repetition is the soul of pedagogy—and of rigorous scholarship. This research, born of countless sleepless nights, has the potential to revolutionize procedural animation and contribute to the betterment of humankind.
